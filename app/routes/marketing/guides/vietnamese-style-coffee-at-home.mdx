Vietnamese coffee is one of the simplest brewing setups in the world - a single small metal dripper, hot water, and a spoonful of condensed milk - and yet it produces one of the most distinctive cups going. The whole thing rides on one piece of equipment and one type of bean.

## The Phin Filter

A phin (Vietnamese coffee filter, sometimes called a Vietnamese coffee dripper, brewer or strainer) is a small single-cup metal filter that sits directly on top of a glass or cup. It has four parts: a perforated base dish, a cylindrical chamber that holds the grounds, a perforated press disc that sits on top of the grounds, and a lid.

Unlike a South Indian filter's percolating drip through a tall column of coffee, a phin brews as a shallow, wide bed - a design suited to brewing one cup directly to order rather than a batch of decoction.

## How to Brew Ca Phe Sua Da / Ca Phe Sua Nong

1. Spoon 2-3 tablespoons of sweetened condensed milk into the bottom of a glass or cup.
2. Set the phin on top, add 2-3 tablespoons of coarsely-ground dark roast coffee (Robusta is traditional - more on why below), and shake gently to level it.
3. Place the perforated press disc on top of the grounds, pressing lightly.
4. Pour a small amount of hot water first to let the grounds bloom for 20-30 seconds, then fill the chamber and put the lid on.
5. Let it drip slowly - a full brew takes 4-5 minutes for one cup.
6. Once done, stir the coffee into the condensed milk at the bottom. For **ca phe sua nong** (hot milk coffee), drink as is. For **ca phe sua da** (iced milk coffee), pour over a full glass of ice.

## Why Dark-Roast Robusta Suits This Method

Vietnamese coffee is traditionally made with Robusta, roasted dark, and this isn't a random regional preference - it's a deliberate pairing with the condensed milk.

- **Robusta is naturally stronger and more bitter** than Arabica, with roughly double the caffeine. That bitterness is exactly what's needed to cut through a generous spoonful of sweet condensed milk without the cup tasting like dessert.
- **A dark roast pushes bitterness and body further**, adding a slight char note that holds up even after being diluted with milk and ice.
- **Arabica, by contrast, is smoother and more delicate** - brewed the same way with condensed milk, its subtler notes tend to get completely buried under the sweetness, so it doesn't perform as well in this specific recipe even though it's excellent in other brewing methods.

This is a good example of "best bean" being method-dependent rather than universal: a bright, fruity Arabica that shines in a pour-over would taste flat and one-note here, while the same dark Robusta that seems harsh brewed alone through a filter comes into its own against condensed milk.

## Getting the Grind and Ratio Right

A phin needs a coarser grind than South Indian filter coffee - closer to what you'd use in a French press. Too fine and the water won't drip through the perforated disc at all; too coarse and the coffee comes out weak and watery. If your drip stalls, loosen the press disc slightly rather than switching to a finer grind.
