"Drip coffee" describes a family of brewing methods that all share one principle - water passes through grounds once, pulled by gravity, and drips out the bottom as coffee. Beyond that shared principle, electric, pour-over, and cold drip machines work quite differently.

## Electric Drip Coffee Makers

This is the countertop machine most people picture: water reservoir, heating element, and a basket that holds a paper or reusable filter over a carafe. An automatic drip coffee maker heats water to near-boiling, releases it over the grounds in stages, and lets it drip through into the pot below - typically finishing a full pot in 4-6 minutes.

Some electric drip coffee makers with a built-in grinder handle the whole process from whole bean to brewed pot, grinding fresh right before water hits the grounds - useful if freshly-ground coffee matters to you but you don't want a separate grinder to manage.

**Good for:** brewing a full pot at once for multiple people, minimal hands-on effort, consistent results once dialled in.

## Pour-Over Drip

A pour-over dripper (V60-style cone, Chemex, or similar) is the manual version of the same idea - you pour hot water over grounds by hand, usually in a slow, circular motion, and it drips through a paper or metal filter into a cup or carafe below. No machine, no reservoir, no automation.

**Good for:** full control over water temperature, pour rate and timing, which is why it's the preferred method for people dialling in a specific bean's flavour. Slower and more hands-on than electric drip, brewing one or two cups at a time rather than a full pot.

## Cold Drip / Cold Brew Towers

A cold drip coffee maker (also called a cold brew tower or Kyoto-style dripper) uses the same gravity-drip principle but with room-temperature or iced water, dripped extremely slowly - often one drop every second or two - over several hours. This is different from cold brew steeping (soaking grounds in cold water for 12-24 hours and then filtering), though both fall under "cold brew" loosely. A cold drip tower produces a clean, low-acidity concentrate through slow drip rather than steeping.

**Good for:** a smoother, less acidic concentrate than hot brewing, at the cost of a much longer brew time and a dedicated (often decorative) piece of equipment.

## Percolators Are a Different Method Entirely

Worth separating out since "drip pot coffee maker" and "percolator" get used interchangeably in casual conversation, but they're mechanically different. A percolator boils water in a base chamber, forces it up a tube, and lets it cascade back down over the grounds repeatedly - recirculating the same water multiple times. Drip methods, by contrast, pass water through the grounds once. Percolated coffee tends to be stronger and can turn bitter or over-extracted if left percolating too long; drip coffee's single pass gives more consistent, controllable extraction.

## What to Look For

- **Household size** - electric drip for multiple cups at once, pour-over for one or two people who want more control.
- **Time you're willing to spend** - electric drip and percolators are largely hands-off; pour-over and cold drip both demand patience, just on very different timescales (minutes vs hours).
- **What you're actually optimising for** - a clean, bright cup favours pour-over; a smooth, low-acid concentrate favours cold drip; convenience favours electric.

## The Filter Method Doesn't Fix Bad Coffee

Every drip method - electric, pour-over, or cold - extracts whatever's in the grounds. A coarse, stale, or poorly-matched bean will taste flat no matter how well-engineered the dripper is.
