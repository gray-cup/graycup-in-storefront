Decoction is the whole game in filter coffee. Everything else - the milk, the sugar, the froth - just carries it. A weak or bitter decoction can't be fixed downstream, so it's worth getting the process itself right.

## The Filter Coffee Making Process, Step by Step

**1. Grind.** Filter coffee needs a fine, slightly gritty grind - finer than what you'd use for a French press, but not as fine as espresso. If you're buying whole beans, ask for a filter coffee grind specifically; commercial coffee is usually pre-ground to the right size.

**2. Load the top chamber.** Add 2-3 tablespoons of powder for a standard filter, spread it evenly, then press down gently with the perforated disc. Firm and level, not compressed - packing it too tight slows the drip to a crawl or stops it altogether; too loose and water rushes through before it can extract properly.

**3. Add water, just off the boil.** Water that's actually boiling can scald the grounds and pull out bitterness; water that's too cool under-extracts and leaves the decoction thin. Just off the boil - a minute or two after it stops rolling - is the target. Fill the chamber, close the lid to trap heat and slow evaporation.

**4. Let it drip - don't rush it.** A full drip takes 15-20 minutes. This is the step everyone tries to shortcut, and it's the one that matters most. Rushing it (by using a coarser grind or a looser pack to make it drip faster) is the single most common cause of a weak decoction.

**5. Mix and serve.** Combine roughly 1 part decoction to 3-4 parts hot milk, adjusting to taste, sweeten, and pour between tumbler and dabara a few times to froth and cool it to drinking temperature.

## Troubleshooting

- **Decoction too weak** - usually too coarse a grind, too little powder, or water that rushed through in under 10 minutes. Pack slightly firmer and use a finer grind next time.
- **Decoction too bitter** - almost always water that was too hot, or the powder sat packed too tight and over-extracted. Let the water cool slightly before pouring, and ease off on the pressing disc.
- **Drip stops completely** - the grind is too fine or the disc is pressed too hard, sealing the grounds. Loosen the pack.
- **Decoction tastes flat after a day** - decoction oxidises like any brewed coffee. It keeps in the fridge for 2-3 days but is noticeably better used within the first day.

## Decoction vs Bottled Filter Coffee Liquid

Alongside home brewing, there's a whole category of ready-made concentrated filter coffee liquid - sold as bottled or packet "instant filter coffee liquid," with ID being one of the more recognisable names in that space. It's worth understanding what it actually is rather than treating it as a mystery product: a pre-brewed, concentrated coffee extract, similar in concept to home decoction but made in bulk and bottled for shelf life, then diluted with hot milk the same way you'd dilute fresh decoction.

**Where bottled/instant coffee liquid makes sense:**
- No filter on hand, or no time to wait out a 15-20 minute drip.
- Travelling, hostel or office setups where brewing isn't practical.
- Consistent flavour every time, since it's made in a controlled batch.

**Where fresh decoction wins:**
- Flavour and aroma are noticeably better right after brewing - any concentrate loses some volatility in bottling and storage.
- You control the ratio, the roast, and the blend, rather than taking whatever's in the bottle.
- No preservatives or long shelf-life additives to account for.

Neither is "wrong" - they solve different problems. A bottled liquid is a reasonable stand-in when brewing isn't an option; fresh decoction is worth the 20 minutes when you have them.
