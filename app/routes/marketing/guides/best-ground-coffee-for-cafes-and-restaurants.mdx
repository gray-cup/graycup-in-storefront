Buying coffee for a cafe or restaurant is a genuinely different decision than buying a bag for your kitchen. At home, you're optimising for taste on one or two cups a day, brewed carefully. In a food business, you're optimising for a batch that stays consistent across dozens or hundreds of cups a day, made by different staff, at different times, under time pressure - and taste is only one factor in that.

## What Actually Matters at Volume

**Batch-to-batch consistency.** Your regulars will notice if today's filter coffee tastes different from last week's. A supplier who roasts to a fixed recipe every time - not "seasonal" small-batch variation - is more valuable in food service than a bean that's slightly more interesting but inconsistent.

**Bulk and wholesale pricing.** Buying retail-sized bags for a high-volume operation is expensive and means constant reordering. Wholesale pricing on larger quantities (5kg, 10kg, and up) brings your per-cup cost down meaningfully, which matters directly to your margins.

**A blend that holds up to real-world serving conditions.** Coffee that's kept warm on a hot plate, brewed in bulk urns, or pulled through a busy service window behaves differently than a single careful pour-over. A blend that turns bitter or flat quickly under heat or high-volume brewing will cost you more in complaints and re-brews than it saves in price. Robusta-inclusive blends tend to hold body and crema better under these conditions than delicate pure-Arabica lots, which is part of why classic filter-coffee and espresso blends lean on a Robusta or chicory component for food-service use.

**Reliable supply and reorder cadence.** Running out of your house coffee mid-shift is a real operational problem. A supplier who can commit to consistent lead times and reorder quantities is worth more than one who's occasionally cheaper but unpredictable.

## A Quick Buying Checklist

- Ask for a sample batch before committing to a bulk order, and taste it under your actual brewing conditions (urn, hot plate, espresso machine) - not just as a single cup.
- Confirm minimum order quantities and typical lead times up front.
- Check whether the roast profile is fixed or seasonal, and pick fixed if consistency matters more to your customers than novelty.
- Re-evaluate every few months as your volume changes - what works at 20 cups a day may not be the most cost-effective choice at 200.

---

For a cafe or restaurant, the "best" ground coffee isn't necessarily the most interesting one - it's the one that tastes the same every time, holds up to your actual service conditions, and comes with pricing and supply that work at your volume.
