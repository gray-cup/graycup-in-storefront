If you just got your first home espresso machine (or any coffee machine), the coffee you pick matters less than most beginners think - and getting a feel for the machine matters more than chasing the "perfect" bean on day one.

## Start With a Forgiving Medium Roast

Very light roasts are dense and need precise, often finer grinding and higher pressure to extract properly - underextract them even slightly and you get sour, thin shots. Very dark roasts extract faster and can turn bitter or ashy if you overdo the dose or grind too fine. A **medium roast** sits in the middle: it's noticeably more tolerant of small mistakes in grind size, dose, and extraction time, which is exactly what you want while you're still learning your machine's quirks.

## Grind Coarser Than You Think, at First

"True" espresso grind is fine, almost like table salt, but dialling in a brand-new machine at that fineness often produces slow, choked, over-extracted shots before you've learned to judge dose and tamp pressure. It's easier to:

1. Start slightly coarser than the "espresso fine" mark your grinder suggests.
2. Pull a shot and taste it - if it's sour or runs too fast, go a step finer; if it's bitter or runs too slow, go a step coarser.
3. Make small adjustments and re-taste, rather than jumping several settings at once.

This way you're troubleshooting one variable at a time instead of guessing blind at the "correct" fine grind from the start.

## Buy Small Bags While You're Learning

Coffee loses freshness within a few weeks of grinding (faster) or roasting (slower). While you're still dialling in technique, a smaller bag that you'll use up in 1-2 weeks means you're always testing with reasonably fresh coffee, rather than practicing on a large bag that's gone stale halfway through.

## Don't Overthink Origin Yet

Single-origin beans, specific altitude notes, and tasting-note wheels are worth exploring once you've got consistent technique - but they add variables you don't need while you're still learning grind size, dose, and tamping. A solid, consistent blend lets you isolate "is this my technique or the bean?" much more easily.
