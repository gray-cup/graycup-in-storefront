"Latte" means different things depending on where you order it. A few regional styles have become popular well outside their country of origin - the Spanish latte, the Irish latte, Turkish and Vietnamese approaches to milk coffee, and what's often labelled an "American latte." Here's how each one actually differs.

## Spanish Latte

A **Spanish latte** (sometimes "cafe con leche" style, though the two aren't identical) is a latte made with sweetened condensed milk in place of some or all of the regular milk and sugar. The condensed milk gives it a thicker texture and a caramel-like sweetness that a regular latte doesn't have. It works both hot and cold - a **hot Spanish latte** uses steamed milk mixed with condensed milk, while a Spanish iced latte layers espresso over condensed milk and ice (covered in more depth in our iced latte guide). If you like your coffee sweet and rich rather than light, this is usually the easiest style to like immediately.

## Irish Latte vs Irish Coffee

This one causes real confusion, so it's worth being precise. **Irish coffee** is a traditional drink made with hot coffee, whiskey, sugar, and a layer of cream on top - it does contain alcohol. An **"Irish latte"** as sold in most cafes is a different, non-alcoholic drink: a regular latte flavoured with an Irish-cream-style syrup (vanilla, caramel, and cream notes similar to Irish cream liqueur but without the alcohol). Whether a specific "Irish coffee latte" on a menu contains alcohol depends entirely on the cafe - some make an actual whiskey-spiked version, most use flavoured syrup only. If it matters to you, ask before ordering rather than assuming either way.

## Turkish-Style Milk Coffee

Turkish coffee itself is traditionally black - very finely ground coffee simmered (not brewed through a filter) in a small pot called a cezve, served unfiltered with the grounds settling at the bottom. A "Turkish latte" isn't a standard traditional drink, but where it appears on menus it usually means Turkish-style coffee with steamed milk added, keeping the fine-grind, simmered brewing method but softening the intensity with milk.

## Vietnamese-Style Coffee

Vietnamese coffee is typically brewed strong through a small metal drip filter (phin) directly over sweetened condensed milk, then served hot or over ice. It's closer in spirit to a Spanish latte in that condensed milk does the sweetening, but the brewing method and the very dark-roast, often Robusta-heavy coffee used give it a distinct, bold flavour. ## American Latte

"American latte" generally refers to a lighter, more diluted version of a standard latte - more milk relative to espresso, sometimes made by adding hot water as well (closer to how an Americano dilutes espresso, but with milk instead of just water). It's less a fixed international style and more a description of ratio: less concentrated, milder coffee flavour per cup.

## Making These at Home

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does an Irish latte contain alcohol?**
Usually not - most cafe "Irish lattes" use a non-alcoholic Irish-cream-flavoured syrup. Actual Irish coffee, made with whiskey, is a separate traditional drink; check with the cafe if you need to be sure which one you're getting.

**What's the difference between a Spanish latte and a regular latte?**
A Spanish latte uses sweetened condensed milk instead of regular milk and sugar, giving it a thicker, sweeter result than a standard latte made with fresh milk.

**Is Vietnamese coffee the same as a latte?**
Not exactly - it's brewed through a phin filter directly over condensed milk rather than made from espresso and steamed milk, but it serves a similar role as a sweet, milky coffee drink.

Spanish, Irish, Turkish, Vietnamese and "American" lattes all take the same core idea - coffee plus milk - and adjust it for local taste, whether that's condensed milk, a flavoured syrup, or a different brew method entirely. None of them require special equipment beyond what you'd use for a regular latte.
